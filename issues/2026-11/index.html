Congratulations, Dr. Scott Heggen: Sloan Grant Opens a Path from Berea to Graduate School

By Mary Robert Garrett, Associate Provost and Dean of Faculty

Dr. Scott Heggen, Associate Professor of Computer Science

Berea College has received a two-year grant from the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation to help computer science students explore graduate school. The project, "Catalyzing Graduate Success: A Fully-Funded Research Pathway for Economically Disadvantaged Students in Computer Science," creates a formal pathway between Berea and the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC).

Dr. Scott Heggen, Associate Professor of Computer Science, leads the project with Dr. Emily Lovell of UCSC. The two have taught together at Berea before.

Starting in summer 2027, six of our students each year will join a fully funded 13-week program. Students will first learn about graduate school remotely. Next, they will join Berea's eight-week Summer Software Development Program and build open-source software. They will finish with three weeks at UCSC, where they will do research with faculty and graduate student mentors and present their results to computer science faculty.

We know our students well. Many work through the summer to support their families, and few have had a chance to see what graduate school involves. This grant removes those barriers. It pays for stipends, housing, meals, and travel so students can focus on learning. It also adds help with graduate applications in the fall.

Our students will leave with research experience, mentors, and a clear picture of graduate school. That will help them make an informed choice about their next step.

Congratulations, again, to Dr. Heggen and Dr. Lovell! We're proud of you and excited for our students!

Educational Studies Earns Full CAEP Accreditation Through 2033

Office of Academic Affairs

Berea College's Educational Studies Department has earned full accreditation from the Council for the Accreditation of Educator Preparation (CAEP). The Accreditation Council found that the department met all seven CAEP standards, with no areas for improvement and no stipulations. That's the best possible outcome of a review.

The accreditation runs through Dec. 31, 2033. It covers all 13 of Berea's initial teacher licensure programs, from elementary education to K-12 music, health and physical education, and secondary science, mathematics, English and social studies. Berea first earned this accreditation in 1998.

This result reflects years of careful, collaborative work and, most of all, the strength of the teachers Berea prepares for the communities they serve.

Thank you to Lisa Rosenbarker, Maggie Robillard, Jon Saderholm, Julian Viera Jr., Keesha Greer-Effs, Michelle Lemmon, Michelle Thornton Adler and Angie Cox for leading this effort. Thank you as well to the faculty, staff, students, alumni and school partners who contributed along the way.

Please join us in congratulating Educational Studies!

Connecting Student Work to Learning Outcomes

By Rob Smith, Executive Director of Institutional Effectiveness and Accreditation

I hope the fall term is treating you well as we head into its final weeks! I know the grading piles are getting taller as the days get shorter, so I'll keep this one brief (by my standards, anyway).

Now is a great time to think about the student "artifacts" (i.e., the actual work students produce in your courses) you might want to collect for your department's annual assessment report. Much of that work is likely being turned in over the next few weeks, and it's a whole lot easier to set aside a batch of papers in December than to go hunting for them in April/May. You probably don't need to create anything new, since the best evidence usually comes from assignments you're already giving. A few possibilities to look for:

  • Capstone papers or projects. Pull a sample (or all of them, if your major is small), remove the names, and have two colleagues score them with a short rubric built around an SLO on written communication or disciplinary analysis.
  • Lab reports or research proposals. These suit SLOs about research design or interpreting data, since a rubric row for each skill shows which ones students have and which are still developing.
  • Embedded exam questions. Tie a handful of final exam items to a content-knowledge SLO and look at the percentage of students who answer each one correctly.
  • Presentations, performances, or portfolios. Score them live with a rubric, or record them to review later.

For our students, this matters because their own work is the most direct evidence we have of what they can do by the time they leave us. A thoughtfully calibrated rubric score on a senior paper tells us more about where students are thriving, and where they're struggling, than course grades alone.

If you're not sure where in your curriculum to look, the Curriculum Mapping Tool I previewed with everyone earlier this fall can help. It's a single HTML file that opens in any browser window and comes pre-populated with your SLOs and courses. Your map doesn't need to be finished to be useful. Even a rough first pass at where each SLO is introduced, reinforced, and mastered will point you toward the courses in those last two categories, which are usually where students have had enough practice to show what they've learned. You can download it here: Curriculum Mapping Tool.

I'd encourage you to take a few minutes at your next department meeting to decide which SLOs you're assessing this year and which assignments you'll collect for them. Please feel free to reach out to me directly if you'd like to talk through your options in more detail.

Ansel Elkins Wins 2026 Granum Foundation Prize

By Mary Robert Garrett, Associate Provost and Dean of Faculty

Congratulations to our colleague Ansel Elkins, who has won the 2026 Granum Foundation Prize for her poetry collection Dirt Eater. The poems explore growing up poor and Latina in rural Alabama with a single mother the community has cast out. Ecco will publish the book in fall 2027.

The Granum Foundation awards the $5,000 prize each year to help a writer finish a book in progress. Poets, fiction writers, and creative nonfiction writers across the U.S. compete for it, and finalists make up roughly the top 1% of entries. Past winners include poet Nicole Sealey.

Dirt Eater is Elkins's second book. Her first, Blue Yodel, won the Yale Younger Poets Prize.

Be sure to congratulate her when you see her on campus.

VitalSource Vitals: Timelines for Adoptions, Why?

By Shannon Purtee

Adoptions for the spring term were due on Nov. 3, 2026. The deadline before VitalSource turns off our ability to add adoptions without taking extra steps (they remove some of our access, and we then have to email them to add adoptions) is Nov. 22, 2026. This is to give VitalSource time to order the physical books to have in their stock to ship to students, and time to acquire an adequate number of eBook and courseware codes from publishers. When we miss our deadlines, it makes it difficult for VitalSource to meet the timeline needed to get students the materials they need. You can see our statistics as of Nov. 3 here.

As you can see, we have many that still need to confirm their adoptions, even if to indicate no purchases are required and direct students to the class syllabus for material lists. It is truly vital to announce in your syllabus and in class that each and every student should log into the bookstore to view their courses. The only place students can correct errors, opt out of programs, and ensure courses are loading correctly is within the VitalSource link, even if they don't plan to purchase anything. Here is a document you can include with your syllabus, email to your students, or have posted/available in class:

Bookstore Ordering Instructions for Students

If you need guidance or support with your adoptions, please reach out to purtees@berea.edu and/or adoptions@vitalsource.com.

Agentic AI Is Already Here. Now What?

By Rob Smith, Executive Director of Institutional Effectiveness and Accreditation

I recently read an article posted by Lance Eaton titled "The Agentic Turn: A Recent Talk." In the article, Eaton asks whether higher ed is about to repeat with agentic AI what it did with generative AI: spend years worrying and criticizing without talking much about how to actually work with it. This fall marks four years since generative AI has been widely available. Remarkably, we've now already graduated students who went through what Eaton calls "curriculum roulette," where the rules about AI changed from one class to the next.

The difference between the two, as Eaton describes it, is that with generative AI, you ask it something and it answers. Simple enough. But with agentic AI, you can give it a goal, and it makes a plan, uses different tools and systems to carry it out, and then tells you when it's done.

But his examples of what this new era of agentic AI looks like in practice are, at once, alarming and inspiring. At the very least, this topic should be occupying more space in our discussions of how AI might manifest itself in our classrooms and on our campuses. His examples got me thinking about what this could look like for me. For instance, imagine I signed up for an online woodworking course with dozens of how-to videos, tool guides, project plans, and safety checklists spread across a bunch of different pages. Instead of having to click around for what might be hours trying to figure out what I need for my first project, I could simply hand all of it to an AI agent and ask it to sort everything by project and skill level, then build me one place to watch, read, take notes, and keep track of the material I've finished. In fact, Eaton did something like this with a real course, and it took him less than an hour.

Another of his more sobering examples, however, is how he gave an agent an essay he'd already written and had the agent retype the essay into a Google Doc over the span of two hours. When Eaton checked back, the version history looked just like it would if a real person had written a real draft. So much for version history as a means of discerning whether a real person wrote something.

It probably doesn't surprise anyone who knows me that I tend to agree with most of what he says in the post. But it feels especially relevant now that we find ourselves working out just how Berea wants to approach AI. His advice at the end, in my opinion, is a good place for us to start:

  • Ask what kind of work the AI is doing. Is it handling routine tasks, analyzing information, or shaping how students learn? The closer it gets to teaching and learning, the more human judgment it needs.
  • We need to set up rules and a safe place to try things out before agents are already running in our systems.
  • Instead of just saying "keep a human in the loop," ask who's responsible for what the AI produces, and how we'll check that work.

You can read his full post here: The Agentic Turn: A Recent Talk. I'd love to hear your thoughts on this if you're willing to share. Alternatively, I'd encourage you to share them with colleagues who are (hopefully) thinking about some of the same questions.
